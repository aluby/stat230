Today: how “good” is our model?

Two definitions for “good”


  1. The model is appropriate and our inferences are valid


  1. The X variable does a good job at explaining the Y variable

Conditions for inference

Our model must be valid in order for our p-values or confidence intervals to be valid:

\(y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i\) where \(\varepsilon_i \overset{\rm iid}{\sim} N (0, \sigma^2)\)


Conditions to check:

  • Linear relationship is appropriate
  • Independent and identically distributed errors (iid)
  • Normally distributed errors
  • Errors have Equal variance

Residuals

Definition: \(e_i =\widehat{\varepsilon}_i = y_i - \widehat{y}_i\)


Properties:

  • sum to zero \(\Longrightarrow\) mean is 0

  • uncorrelated with \(x\) and \(\widehat{y}\)

  • normally distributed

  • \(SD(e_i) = \widehat{\sigma} \sqrt{1 - \dfrac{1}{n} - \dfrac{(x_i - \overline{x})^2}{\sum (x_i - \overline{x})^2}}\)

What happens the conditions aren’t valid?

  • Linearity: if nonlinear, everything breaks!

  • Independence: estimates are still unbiased (i.e. we fit the right line) but measures of the accuracy of those estimates (the SEs) are typically too small

  • Normality: estimates are still unbiased (i.e. we fit the right line), SEs are correct BUT prediction intervals (which rely on the data points being normally distribued around the mean) are wrong

  • Equal variance: estimates are still unbiased but standard errors are wrong (and we don’t know how wrong)

What do we do if our assumptions are violated?

  1. Change our assumptions (hard, need more stats)

  2. Transform \(y\), \(x\), or both

  3. Change the type of inference (remember the bootstrap?)

Is the X variable a “good” predictor of the Y variable?

\(R^2\)

\[\begin{align*} \sum(Y - \bar{Y})^2 &= \sum (\hat{Y} -\bar{Y})^2 \hspace{.9cm} + \sum e_i^2 \\ \text{total variability} &= \text{variability of } \hat{Y}\text{'s} + \text{variability of residuals}\\ \end{align*}\]

Then:

\[R^2 = \frac{\sum (\hat{Y} - \bar{Y})^2}{\sum (Y - \bar{Y})} = \frac{\text{variability of } \widehat{Y}}{\text{variability of }Y} \]

\(R^2\)

The \(R^2\) value gives the proportion of total variability in Y explained by the SLR model

SSNBATNT

  • \(r\): correlation coefficient
  • R: statistical programming language
  • \(R^2\): “coefficient of variation”
  • \(\rho\): population correlation coefficient
  • \(p\): a population proportion
  • p-value: P(test stat | hypothesis)
  • P(A): probability of event A
  • \(\widehat{p}\): sample proportion

Properties of \(R^2\)

  • Between 0 and 1
  • Big when \(\sigma\) is small relative to the overall variability
    • big proportion of variability explained by the model
  • For SLR, \(R^2 = r^2\)
    • (\(r\) = correlation coefficient between X and Y)
