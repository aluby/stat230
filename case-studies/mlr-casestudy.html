Case Study 2: MLR

Overview

The goals of this case study are twofold:

  1. To gain experience with model selection for multiple regression
  2. To present complex results in an easy-to-digest manner

There are two options for this short report: (a) investigating the relationship between pollution and mortality OR (b) modeling Nascar driver ratings.

You will submit your work to this case study in two parts:

  1. individual submission as part of HW6 (due Friday of Week 6 at beginning of class)
    • It’s OK with me if your group works on this together as long as you actually do it together. You should not send code back and forth to complete this portion of the project.
    • You will have in-class time on Friday to work together, so I encourage you to get started on the analysis before class to use your time effectively
  2. a group report (due beginning of class on Wed of Week 7)
    • Your groups are your in-class groups. If you are in a group of 3, you should all work together. If you are in a group of four, you should split into two groups of two.

You should all do an equal amount of contribution to parts (1) and (2). Equal does not necessarily mean identical, but all group members should have a deep understanding of the statistical approaches used and have contributed to the writing of the report.

Project Prompt

Option 1: Does pollution kill people?1

The following dataset consists of 15 variables for each of 60 cities between the years 1959-1961.

  • Response variable: Total age-adjusted mortality from all causes, in deaths per 100,000 population
  • Possible predictors include weather, pollution, and demographic features of each city (described in more detail below).

We wish to investigate the relationship between the pollution variables and mortality, after the other climate and socioeconomic variables are accounted for. (Note: these data may suffer from some spatial dependence. If you come across this, you should note it in your model but do not need to “fix” it)

library(Sleuth3)

pollution <- ex1217

Variables:

  • CITY a character vector indicating the city
  • Mortality total age-adjusted mortality from all causes
  • Precip mean annual precipitation (inches)
  • Humidity percent relative humidity (annual average at 1:00pm)
  • JanTemp mean January temperature (degrees F)
  • JulyTemp mean July temperature (degrees F)
  • Over65 percentage of the population aged 65 years or over
  • House population per household
  • Educ median number of school years completed for persons 25 years or older
  • Sound percentage of the housing that is sound with all facilities
  • Density population density (in persons per square mile of urbanized area)
  • NonWhite percentage of population that is nonwhite
  • WhiteCol percentage of employment in white collar occupations
  • Poor percentage of households with annual income under $3,000 in 1960
  • HC relative pollution potential of hydrocarbons
  • NOX relative pollution potential of oxides of nitrogen
  • SO2 relative pollution potential of sulfur dioxide

Some questions your report should address:

  • How is pollution, or potential pollution as measured by NOX, SO2, and HC, associated with mortality after accoutning for weather and socioeconomic/demographic factors?
  • Which, if any, pollution variables have the strongest association with mortality after accounting for wetaher and other socioeconomic factors?
  • Does your model suggest that stronger regulations on potential pollutants could reduce mortality?

Things to watch out for:

  • Consider transformations
  • It may be helpful to first find a model for mortality that only uses weather and demographic facotrs, adn then add in the pollution variables and perform fruther checks and refinement
  • Watch out for outliers!

A complete analysis will:

  • address the questions that are posed
  • consider transformations and check model assumptions prior to inference
  • consider outlier effects prior to inference and only remove cases that have a justifiable reason for removal – if outliers are discussed, refer to them by city name and explain why they are unusual cases and, if applicable, why you chose to remove them from your model/data
  • refine the model (i.e. reduce the number of model predictors) to address the questions of interest – use appropriate regression tools taught in class (tests, etc) to justify the remove of predictors

Option 2: NASCAR driver ratings (again)

In NASCAR, the Driver Rating is a composite metric used to evaluate overall performance. It provides a comprehensive assessment of a driver’s competitiveness, efficiency, and consistency by combining multiple factors—such as wins, finish position, and average running position—into a single numerical value.

The exact formula has evolved over time, but the goal of this project is to build a statistical approximation of this metric using season-level data from 2007–2022. While consistency (Average Position) is a major factor, we want to investigate how much “extra” value is added by aggressive performance metrics like leading laps or setting fastest laps, after accounting for a driver’s typical finishing position.

To load the NASCAR driver dataset:

nascar <- read.csv("https://aluby.github.io/stat230/data/nascar_driver_statistics.csv")

Variables of interest:

  • Response: DriverRating (The season average of race-by-race ratings)
  • Consistency Predictors: AvgPos (Average running position), AvgFinish (Average finishing position)
  • Performance Predictors: Wins, LapsLed, FastestLaps, QualityPasses (Passes of cars in the Top 15 while under green flag conditions)

(Note: there are other predictors as well! I suggest doing a bit of background reading to understand what these variables mean.)

Your report should address the following:

  • How are performance-based metrics—specifically LapsLed and FastestLaps—associated with a driver’s holistic DriverRating after accounting for their consistency?
  • Which specific performance variables have the strongest association with the rating once consistency is controlled for?
  • Given that many of these variables (like AvgPos and AvgFinish) measure similar things, how does multicollinearity affect the stability of your model?

Things to watch out for:

  • Consider transformations!
  • Many of the variables measure similar things. Be aware of multicollinearity.
  • It may be helpful to first find a model using only “consistency” variables, then add “performance” variables
  • consider outlier effects prior to inference and only remove cases that have a justifiable reason for removal – if outliers are discussed, refer to them by city name and explain why they are unusual cases and, if applicable, why you chose to remove them from your model/data

A Complete Analysis Will:

  • Address the research questions
  • Check MLR conditions and use transformations if necessary
  • Evaluate leverage and influence: Identify specific drivers who are “unusual” cases.
    • Refer to them by name and explain why they might be exerting high leverage on your model.
  • Refine the model: Use model selection tools to reduce the number of predictors, ensuring you keep only those that provide explanatory power
  • Interpret results in context: Provide a clear summary of how “aggressive” driving (leading laps/fastest laps) impacts a driver’s rating compared to “safe” driving (consistent finishing).

Format

  • 4 pages or less (including figures but not including references). You may include up to 2 pages of appendices that do not count towards this limit.
  • Introduction: this is where you introduce the problem and why it is important to study. This should focus on the data and the “real-world” questions.
  • EDA: this is where you discuss the variables of interest and present your EDA (e.g. graphs) to motivate your model
  • Results: this is where you present your model selection approach, final model, and interpret the results. You should explain your results both statistically and in context.
  • Discussion: this is a shorter section that summarizes the main points of your analysis and explains your answer to the research question.
  • Appendices: this is an optional section where you can include graphs or results that you used, but aren’t central to the point you are trying to make in the remainder of the report. (Eg residual plots that were “normal” or a list of transformations that you considered)
