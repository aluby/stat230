Case Study 2: MLR

Overview

The goals of this case study are twofold:

  1. To gain experience building an explanatory multiple regression model, including choosing among candidate models
  2. To present complex results in an easy-to-digest manner

There are two options for this short report: (a) investigating whether green space is associated with life expectancy OR (b) modeling MLB attendance.

You will submit your work to this case study in two parts:

  1. individual submission as part of HW6 (due Friday of Week 6 at beginning of class)
    • It’s OK with me if your group works on this together as long as you actually do it together. You should not send code back and forth to complete this portion of the project.
    • You will have in-class time on Friday to work together, so I encourage you to get started on the analysis before class to use your time effectively
  2. a group report (due beginning of class on Wed of Week 7)
    • Your groups are your in-class groups. If you are in a group of 3, you should all work together. If you are in a group of four, you should split into two groups of two.

You should all do an equal amount of contribution to parts (1) and (2). Equal does not necessarily mean identical, but all group members should have a deep understanding of the statistical approaches used and have contributed to the writing of the report.

Building your model

Both options ask an explanatory question: how is a variable of interest associated with the response after accounting for other variables? Follow the recipe from Day 15:

  1. Start with the question. Sort the predictors into (a) the variable(s) of interest and (b) the variables you need to adjust for. Use background reading to decide what belongs in (b). These variables stay in your model even if they are not “significant.”
  2. Look before you fit. Use EDA to check for transformations and for predictors that are highly correlated with each other.
  3. Fit a small set of candidate models (3–5 models). Do not fit every possible combination, and do not remove predictors one at a time based on their p-values.
  4. Compare the candidates. Use extra sums of squares \(F\) tests for nested models and adjusted \(R^2\)/AIC/BIC for any models. If the tools disagree, explain which you chose and why.
  5. Check the conditions for the model you choose, including unusual and influential cases.
  6. Interpret and justify your choice in context.

Your Results section must include a table of your candidate models (like the one from Day 15) that lists the predictors in each model and the comparison tools you used.

Project Prompt

Option 1: Does green space improve health?

Cities and counties spend a lot of money on parks, trails, and recreation centers, and one argument for this spending is that access to places to be active improves public health. Of course, counties with more parks also tend to be wealthier, more urban, and better educated, and all of those are associated with health too. Your job is to estimate how access to green space is associated with life expectancy after accounting for socioeconomic and demographic differences between counties.

The data come from the County Health Rankings & Roadmaps 2025 data release (University of Wisconsin Population Health Institute). There is one row for each of 989 U.S. counties with a population of at least 50,000.

greenspace <- read.csv("https://aluby.github.io/stat230/data/county_greenspace.csv")

Variables:

  • fips, state, county identify the county
  • region Census region (Northeast, South, North Central, West)
  • population county population
  • Response: life_expectancy average number of years people are expected to live
  • Variables of interest:
    • pct_park_access percentage of the population living within a half mile of a park
    • pct_exercise_access percentage of the population with adequate access to locations for physical activity (parks, gyms, recreation centers, etc.)
  • Other possible predictors:
    • median_income median household income (dollars)
    • pct_child_poverty percentage of people under age 18 in poverty
    • pct_some_college percentage of adults ages 25–44 with some post-secondary education
    • pct_uninsured percentage of the population under age 65 without health insurance
    • pct_unemployed percentage of the population ages 16 and older unemployed but seeking work
    • pct_housing_problems percentage of households with at least one severe housing problem (overcrowding, high housing costs, or lack of kitchen or plumbing facilities)
    • pct_65_older percentage of the population ages 65 and older
    • pct_rural percentage of the population living in a census-defined rural area
    • pct_nonwhite percentage of the population that does not identify as non-Hispanic white

See the 2025 data dictionary and technical documentation for more details on how each measure is defined and which years it comes from.

Your report should address the following:

  • How is access to green space associated with life expectancy after accounting for socioeconomic and demographic differences between counties?
  • pct_park_access and pct_exercise_access measure similar things. Which one (or both?) belongs in your model, and how does that choice affect your answer?
  • Does your model suggest that increasing access to parks or exercise opportunities would increase life expectancy? (Think carefully about what an observational, county-level study can tell us.)

Things to watch out for:

  • Consider transformations (especially for money or count-related variables)
  • It may be helpful to first find a model for life expectancy that only uses the socioeconomic and demographic variables, and then add the green space variables
  • Many of the socioeconomic variables are correlated with each other. Be aware of multicollinearity.
  • Watch out for outliers! If you discuss unusual counties, refer to them by name and look into why they are unusual
  • Counties near each other tend to be similar, so these data may suffer from some spatial dependence. If you come across this, note it as a limitation but you do not need to “fix” it

Option 2: Do fans reward winning?

Ticket sales are a major source of revenue for Major League Baseball teams, so front offices care a lot about what brings fans to the ballpark. Is it winning this season? Building a reputation by winning last season? Or exciting baseball, like home runs and stolen bases? Your job is to estimate how team success is associated with attendance after accounting for other differences between teams and seasons.

The data come from the Lahman Baseball Database (available in the Lahman R package). There is one row for each MLB team in each season from 2012–2019 and 2022–2023 (300 team-seasons). The 2020 season (no fans) and 2021 season (capacity limits) are not included.

mlb <- read.csv("https://aluby.github.io/stat230/data/mlb_attendance.csv")

Variables:

  • team team name, franchise franchise ID (use this to follow a team across seasons, since some teams changed names)
  • Response: attendance_per_game average home attendance per game
  • Variables of interest:
    • win_pct proportion of games won this season
    • prev_win_pct proportion of games won the previous season
    • prev_playoffs 1 if the team made the playoffs the previous season, 0 otherwise
  • Other possible predictors:
    • year season
    • league American League (AL) or National League (NL)
    • division East (E), Central (C), or West (W)
    • runs_per_game runs scored per game
    • hr_per_game home runs hit per game
    • sb_per_game stolen bases per game
    • runs_allowed_per_game runs allowed per game
    • strikeouts_per_game strikeouts by the team’s pitchers per game
    • park_factor ballpark factor for batters (100 = neutral; above 100 means the park favors hitters)

Your report should address the following:

  • How is a team’s success (this season and last season) associated with attendance after accounting for other differences between teams and seasons?
  • Is attendance more strongly associated with winning this season or with winning last season?
  • After accounting for winning, is “exciting” baseball (home runs, stolen bases, strikeouts) associated with attendance?

Things to watch out for:

  • Consider transformations, but only use one if it improves the residual plots (check the residuals, not just the shape of each variable)
  • Winning percentage is largely determined by runs scored and runs allowed. Be aware of multicollinearity and think about which of these variables answer your question
  • It may be helpful to first find a model using only the “other” variables, and then add the success variables
  • Watch out for outliers! If you discuss unusual team-seasons, refer to them by team and year and look into why they are unusual
  • Each team appears in the data many times, so these observations are not fully independent. If you come across this, note it as a limitation but you do not need to “fix” it

A complete analysis will (both options):

  • address the questions that are posed
  • consider transformations and check model assumptions prior to inference
  • consider outlier effects prior to inference and only remove cases that have a justifiable reason for removal
    • if outliers are discussed, refer to them by name and explain why they are unusual cases and, if applicable, why you chose to remove them from your model/data
  • compare a small set of candidate models (see Building your model) and justify your final model using both the comparison tools and the context of the question
  • interpret the coefficients for your variables of interest, with confidence intervals, in context

Format

  • 4 pages or less (including figures but not including references). You may include up to 2 pages of appendices that do not count towards this limit.
  • Introduction: this is where you introduce the problem and why it is important to study. This should focus on the data and the “real-world” questions.
  • EDA: this is where you discuss the variables of interest and present your EDA (e.g. graphs) to motivate your model. It may be a good place to introduce transformations.
  • Results: this is where you present your candidate models, your model selection approach, your final model, and interpret the results. You should explain your results both statistically and in context.
  • Discussion: this is a shorter section that summarizes the main points of your analysis and explains your answer to the research question.
  • Appendices: this is an optional section where you can include graphs or results that you used, but aren’t central to the point you are trying to make in the remainder of the report. (Eg residual plots that were “normal” or a list of transformations that you considered)
