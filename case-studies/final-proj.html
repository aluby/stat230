Final Project

Overview

The final project is your chance to use the methods from this course to answer a question you care about. You will build a logistic (binary or binomial) or Poisson regression model, choose among candidate models, and report your findings in a short, polished paper.

You can either:

  • start from one of the four starter datasets below, or
  • find your own data (see Finding your own data).

You will work in groups of 2 or 3 people assigned by me with your input. The expectation is that each student shares equal responsibility for the report. You will turn in one report per group. Please let me know if you are having trouble communicating with your group.

Due dates:

  • Friday of Week 7: Project parter form
  • Friday of Week 8: Study proposal due (with dataset)
  • Monday of Week 10: Rough draft of paper due (optional)
  • Wednesday of Week 10: Mini-mini poster session
  • Monday of finals: Final report due
    • 1% extra credit for submitting to USCLAP (requires a rough draft due Monday of Week 10 and using a dataset not from our list)
    • 1% extra credit for submitting final report 24 hours early

Stage 1: Study Proposal

Identify the research questions and data that will guide your project. Your dataset should have at least 100 observations/cases and at least 5 variables (at least four predictor variables and a response variable). The response variable should be binary (0/1) or a count variable, and you should plan to use either logistic or Poisson regression. You should not use textbook data. You should carefully consider the questions that you intend to ask with your data, as well as the design of the study and how the data were or will be collected. It is essential that the methods used to analyze your dataset ultimately be appropriate to both the question of interest and the data at hand.

Examples of previous project abstracts will be posted on Moodle to give you an idea of what kinds of things people have done in the past. I will also post some online sources of data (but you may obtain data from a source not listed there).

You should submit:

  1. A paragraph identifying your research question(s) and data. Be explicit about which variable is the response, which predictor(s) are your variables of interest, and which predictors you will adjust for. Describe how the data were collected, with a link or citation.
  • You should not use textbook data for this project. One goal of this project is for you to seek out data from an outside source to answer a question that you are interested in.
  • I discourage you from using data found on Kaggle, since many datasets uploaded to that website do not have appropriate documentation for how they were collected (and often are fake datasets). If you choose to analyze a dataset found on Kaggle, you should be sure that you have appropriate information about where the data came from.
  • Please keep in mind that many datasets have complex structures that require methods that are more advanced that we have learned in this class. A few kinds of data that generally won’t work well for this project because there will usually be a violation of the independence assumption of our models: – Time series data (measurements of a single observational unit over time) – Spatial data – Longitudinal data (data with repeated measurements on the same individual). (Note: this can work if you consider averaging the repeated measurements or only looking at the measurements at one or two time points)
  • If you are using a starter dataset, your proposal should still state your research question. You do not have to use the example questions, and you do not have to use every variable.

  1. One informative and well-labeled plot or table along with a sentence or two describing what it tells you. This is not supposed to be an exhaustive analysis. The purpose is for you to ensure that your data are in a format that is ready for analysis and begin to think about modeling issues that may arise.

  2. A link to your data. The dataset should be in a format readable by R (e.g., .csv).

We will do “data presentations” in a mini-mini poster session on the last day of class.

Stage 2: Final Report

We will follow the USCLAP guidelines for the format of the report: https://www.causeweb.org/usproc/usclap

Building your model

Use the same recipe as Case Study 2:

  1. Start with the question. Identify your variable(s) of interest and the variables you need to adjust for.
  2. Look before you fit. Use EDA to check the form of each quantitative predictor and their relationship with the response
  3. Fit a small set of candidate models (3–5 models).
  4. Compare the candidates with drop-in-deviance tests for nested models and AIC/BIC for any models.
  5. Check the conditions: independence, the form of each predictor, overdispersion (binomial and Poisson), and unusual or influential cases.
  6. Interpret and justify your choice, with coefficients on the odds-ratio or rate-ratio scale and confidence intervals.

Format

  • 3 pages or less (including figures but not including references). You may include up to 2 pages of appendices of graphs or coefficient tables that do not count towards this limit. Note that these should be supporting graphs, not discussed in the main text.
  • Abstract: a one-paragraph summary of the problem and your findings (200 words or fewer).
  • Introduction: introduce the problem and why it is important to study. This should focus on the data and the “real-world” questions. Give enough background that a reader doesn’t need to refer to your sources, but take care not to plagiarize them. Cite sources as needed.
  • Data and EDA: describe how the data were collected and the variables of interest, and present key figures that help the reader understand your data and “see what’s coming” in the Results section.
  • Methods: state your model, your candidate models, and how you chose among them.
  • Results: present your final model and interpret the results, both statistically and in context.
  • Discussion: a shorter section that summarizes the main points of your analysis and answers your research question.
  • Appendices: graphs or results that you used but that aren’t central to your main point (e.g., diagnostic plots, a list of models you considered).

Option 1: Caring for city trees (logistic regression)

Street trees cool neighborhoods, absorb stormwater, and improve air quality, but life on a city sidewalk is hard on a tree. In 2015, more than 2,000 volunteers and NYC Parks staff surveyed every street tree in New York City. They recorded each tree’s species and size, rated its health, and noted damage (for example, wires or lights wrapped around the trunk, or roots trapped by paving stones), signs that residents were caring for it (“stewardship”), and whether it had a guard around it.

  • Source: 2015 Street Tree Census, NYC Open Data (NYC Department of Parks & Recreation). The data dictionary is attached on the dataset page.
trees <- read.csv("https://aluby.github.io/stat230/data/nyc_trees.csv")
  • Unit: one living street tree. To reduce dependence between neighboring trees, the data are a random sample of 10,000 trees with at most one tree per block.
  • Response: good_health (1 = rated in good health, 0 = fair or poor)
  • Possible predictors:
    • diameter trunk diameter at breast height (inches)
    • species the eight most common species, plus other
    • on_curb 1 = planted along the curb, 0 = set back from the curb
    • stewardship signs that people care for the tree (none, 1-2 signs, 3+ signs)
    • guards tree guard (none, helpful, harmful, unsure)
    • sidewalk_damage 1 = the sidewalk next to the tree is damaged (cracked or raised)
    • root_problem 1 = roots constrained by paving stones, a metal grate, or something else
    • trunk_problem 1 = wires, lights, or something else wrapped around the trunk
    • branch_problem 1 = lights, shoes, or something else in the branches
    • borough NYC borough
    • surveyor who rated the tree (volunteer, census staff, or parks staff)

Example questions:

  • Which kinds of damage are most strongly associated with poor tree health, after accounting for species and size?
  • Are trees with signs of stewardship more likely to be healthy?
  • Do volunteers and staff rate tree health differently?

Watch out for: The stewardship and guard associations are small, so think about the size of the effect, not just its p-value. Health is a visual rating by the surveyor, and nearby trees may still be similar.

Option 2: Who graduates from college? (binomial logistic regression)

Colleges vary widely in what fraction of their students finish a degree. Some of this reflects the students a college admits, and some might reflect what the college does. The US Department of Education’s College Scorecard reports, for each institution, how many students in an entering cohort completed a degree within six years, along with information about the students, cost, and resources.

  • Source: College Scorecard, US Department of Education (data documentation)
college <- read.csv("https://aluby.github.io/stat230/data/college_completion.csv")
  • Unit: one public or private nonprofit four-year college (1,341 institutions that mainly award bachelor’s degrees and had an entering cohort of at least 50 full-time, first-time students)
  • Response: completers (number of students in the entering cohort who completed a degree within six years) out of cohort_size
  • Possible predictors:
    • control public or private nonprofit
    • hbcu 1 = historically Black college or university
    • region and locale (city, suburb, town, rural)
    • admission_rate proportion of applicants admitted
    • sat_avg average SAT score of admitted students (missing for 374 schools, many of which are test-optional)
    • pct_pell percentage of undergraduates receiving a Pell grant (a common measure of how many low-income students a college enrolls)
    • enrollment undergraduate enrollment
    • cost average annual cost of attendance (dollars)
    • faculty_salary average monthly faculty salary (dollars)
    • instruction_spending instructional spending per full-time-equivalent student (dollars)
    • pct_fulltime_faculty percentage of faculty who are full-time
    • name and state identify the institution

Example questions:

  • After accounting for selectivity and resources, do colleges that enroll more Pell grant recipients have lower completion rates?
  • Are public and private nonprofit colleges with similar students and resources equally likely to graduate their students?

Watch out for: Colleges differ in many ways that no model captures. Several predictors (enrollment, spending) are very right-skewed. If you use sat_avg, you will lose about a quarter of the colleges, and those colleges are not a random subset.

Option 3: Do crowds sway referees? (Poisson regression)

Home teams win more often in almost every sport, and one explanation is that crowds pressure referees into favoring the home team. When COVID-19 forced soccer leagues to play in empty stadiums, researchers got a rare natural experiment: the teams, players, and referees were the same, but the crowds were gone.

  • Source: football-data.co.uk match results and statistics for the English Premier League
epl <- read.csv("https://aluby.github.io/stat230/data/epl_cards.csv")
  • Unit: one team in one Premier League match, 2017–18 through 2022–23 (2,280 matches, so 4,560 rows: one for the home team and one for the away team)
  • Response: yellow_cards the team received (or fouls the team was called for, which is also a referee decision)
  • Possible predictors:
    • home 1 = home team, 0 = away team
    • fans 1 = fans in the stadium, 0 = no fans (matches from the June 2020 restart through mid-May 2021)
    • fouls and opp_fouls fouls called on the team and on its opponent
    • shots, opp_shots, and corners
    • win_prob and opp_win_prob the betting market’s pre-match probability that the team (or its opponent) wins, a measure of how strong each team was expected to be
    • season, date, referee, team, opponent, and match_id (to link the two rows from the same match)

Example questions:

  • Do away teams receive more yellow cards than home teams? Did this difference shrink when stadiums were empty?
  • Did referees call fouls differently on home and away teams when fans were absent?
  • After accounting for team strength, did matches without fans have fewer cards overall?

Watch out for: the two rows for each match are not independent; consider how this affects your conclusions. Fouls are called by the referee, so think carefully about whether adjusting for them helps or hurts when your question is about referee bias. The no-fans period is only about one season, so some differences may be too small to detect with this much data. That is a legitimate finding if you report it with a confidence interval.

Finding your own data

If you find your own dataset, it should come from a source that documents how the data were collected. Some good places to start:

  • Government data portals: data.gov, CDC data, city open data portals (e.g., Minneapolis, NYC)
  • Surveys: General Social Survey, Pew Research Center, ICPSR
  • Data journalism: The Pudding, ProPublica Data Store, FiveThirtyEight
  • Research data repositories: Dryad, Harvard Dataverse

Some things to keep in mind:

  • You should not use textbook data for your own dataset.
  • I discourage you from using data found on Kaggle, since many datasets uploaded there do not document how they were collected (and some are fake). If you choose a Kaggle dataset, you should find the original source.
  • Many datasets have structures that require methods beyond this class. These kinds of data generally violate the independence assumption of our models:
    • Time series data (measurements of a single observational unit over time)
    • Spatial data
    • Longitudinal data (repeated measurements on the same individual). (Note: this can work if you average the repeated measurements or only look at one or two time points)

Best practices for small group work

You will work in groups of two or three for this project.

While you are permitted to do some work separately (e.g., the homework components, revising a section at a time when your schedules don’t permit meeting together), I discourage you from completing substantial portions of the final report individually. Each partner should contribute substantively to each component of the report, and each partner is assumed to be an equal contributor to the final product. You should be in regular contact with your partner(s) and should meet at least once outside of class. If you are having issues communicating with your partner(s), please email me as soon as possible.

Academic Integrity

For this case study, you may use the following resources:

  • Your own course notes (from any class!) and any Stat230 materials of your own or provided by Amanda
  • Web searches/AI where your search/prompts are general questions about coding (e.g., how do I subset a data set in R where I want only certain numerical values?). If you use AI, you MUST provide a document shows your “conversation.” Failure to do so will be considered academic dishonesty.
  • Web searches/AI where your search/prompts are background research about your project topic. If you use AI, you MUST provide a document shows your “conversation.” You must also find a real source (published by a reputable source, ideally peer-reviewed) and don’t rely purely on AI. Failure to do so will be considered academic dishonesty.
  • Web searches/AI where your search/prompts are general questions about formatting (e.g., how do I make a table in GoogleDocs?). If you use AI, you MUST provide a document shows your “conversation.” Failure to do so will be considered academic dishonesty
  • The writing center for help editing your write up.
  • Your in-class group
  • Amanda
  • Math/Stat Skills Center or the QRC for R coding help or help with statistical models

For this project you may NOT use the following resources:

  • Other humans (including other folks in this class)
  • Web searches/AI where your searches/prompts are the specific questions you are being asked in the project about coding (e.g., How do I fit a linear regression model in R with the sales price as the response variable and square footage as the predictor variable?).
  • Web searches/AI where your search/prompts are the specific formatting questions you are being asked to do for the project (e.g., please write a caption for this table).
  • AI to write or edit any portion of your original write-up or your final write-up.
