1. Express a regression model using proper notation

Example question The fake news data includes negative, the percent of words in the article with negative sentiment, and title_has_excl, whether the title has an exclamation point. The model below allows the relationship between negative and the chance that an article is fake to be different for titles with and without an exclamation point.

news_int <- glm(type_num ~ negative * title_has_excl, data = news, family = "binomial")
tidy(news_int)
# A tibble: 4 × 5
  term                        estimate std.error statistic  p.value
  <chr>                          <dbl>     <dbl>     <dbl>    <dbl>
1 (Intercept)                   -1.95      0.523    -3.72  0.000199
2 negative                       0.404     0.153     2.64  0.00841 
3 title_has_exclTRUE             3.57      2.27      1.57  0.116   
4 negative:title_has_exclTRUE   -0.289     0.562    -0.515 0.607

  1. Using proper statistical notation, write down the theoretical model that was fit, on both the log-odds scale and the probability scale. Define all variables.

  2. Write down the fitted log-odds equation for titles with an exclamation point and for titles without one.

TipSolution
  1. \(Y_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\pi_i)\), where \(Y_i = 1\) if article \(i\) is fake and 0 if it is real, and

\[\log\left(\frac{\pi_i}{1 - \pi_i}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \beta_3 x_{i1} x_{i2}\]

\[\pi_i = \frac{e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \beta_3 x_{i1} x_{i2}}}{1 + e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \beta_3 x_{i1} x_{i2}}}\]

where \(\pi_i\) is the probability that article \(i\) is fake, \(x_{i1}\) is the percent of words with negative sentiment, and \(x_{i2}\) is an indicator variable that equals 1 if the title has an exclamation point and 0 if not. There is no error term: the randomness comes from \(Y_i\) being a 0 or a 1.

  1. Without an exclamation point (\(x_2 = 0\)):

\[\text{logit}(\widehat\pi) = -1.947 + 0.404 \times \text{negative}\]

With an exclamation point (\(x_2 = 1\)):

\[\text{logit}(\widehat\pi) = (-1.947 + 3.575) + (0.404 -0.289) \times \text{negative} = 1.628 + 0.115 \times \text{negative}\]

Example question In the budworm experiment, batches of 20 moths of each sex were exposed to different doses of a toxin, and the number of moths in each batch that died was recorded. The model below uses sex and \(\log_2(\text{dose})\), and their interaction.

bud_int <- glm(cbind(numdead, N - numdead) ~ sex * log2(dose),
               data = budworm, family = "binomial")
tidy(bud_int)
# A tibble: 4 × 5
  term            estimate std.error statistic      p.value
  <chr>              <dbl>     <dbl>     <dbl>        <dbl>
1 (Intercept)       -2.99      0.553    -5.42  0.0000000609
2 sexM               0.175     0.778     0.225 0.822       
3 log2(dose)         0.906     0.167     5.42  0.0000000589
4 sexM:log2(dose)    0.353     0.270     1.31  0.191

  1. What is \(Y_i\), and what is \(n_i\)? Using proper notation, write down the theoretical binomial logistic regression model that was fit. Define all variables.

  2. Write down the fitted log-odds equation for female moths and for male moths.

  3. Why is \(\log_2(\text{dose})\) a convenient scale for this predictor? What does a 1-unit increase in \(\log_2(\text{dose})\) mean in terms of the dose?

TipSolution
  1. \(Y_i\) is the number of moths in batch \(i\) that died, and \(n_i = 20\) is the number of moths in the batch. The model is \(Y_i \sim \text{Binomial}(n_i, \pi_i)\) with

\[\log\left(\frac{\pi_i}{1 - \pi_i}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \beta_3 x_{i1} x_{i2}\]

where \(\pi_i\) is the probability that a moth in batch \(i\) dies, \(x_{i1}\) is an indicator variable that equals 1 if the batch is male and 0 if female, and \(x_{i2} = \log_2(\text{dose})\) for batch \(i\).

  1. Females (\(x_1 = 0\)):

\[\text{logit}(\widehat\pi) = -2.994 + 0.906 \log_2(\text{dose})\]

Males (\(x_1 = 1\)):

\[\text{logit}(\widehat\pi) = (-2.994 + 0.175) + (0.906 + 0.353) \log_2(\text{dose}) = -2.819 + 1.259 \log_2(\text{dose})\]

  1. The doses in the experiment double each time (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32), so they are equally spaced on the \(\log_2\) scale. A 1-unit increase in \(\log_2(\text{dose})\) is a doubling of the dose.

2. Interpret the coefficients of a model in context

Example question Below is a model for whether an applicant was accepted to medical school, using their undergraduate GPA and MCAT score.

med_glm <- glm(Acceptance ~ GPA + MCAT, data = medschool, family = "binomial")
tidy(med_glm)

  <chr>          <dbl>     <dbl>     <dbl>    <dbl>
1 (Intercept)  -22.4       6.45      -3.47 0.000527
2 GPA            4.68      1.64       2.85 0.00439 
3 MCAT           0.165     0.103      1.59 0.111

  1. Interpret the coefficient for GPA on the odds scale, for a 0.1-point increase in GPA.

  2. Interpret the coefficient for MCAT on the odds scale, for a 5-point increase in MCAT score.

TipSolution

  1. \(e^{0.1 \times 4.676} = 1.60\). Holding MCAT score constant, a 0.1-point increase in GPA is associated with the odds of acceptance being multiplied by 1.60 (about a 60% increase in the odds).

  2. \(e^{5 \times 0.165} = 2.28\). Holding GPA constant, a 5-point increase in MCAT score is associated with the odds of acceptance being multiplied by 2.28.

  3. No. The intercept is the log-odds of acceptance for an applicant with a GPA of 0 and an MCAT score of 0, which is far outside the range of the data (GPAs range from 2.72 to 3.97).

3. Check necessary conditions for a regression model and discuss the implications of a model deficiency

Example question Below are the leverage and Cook’s distance plots for the smartphone and sleep model, good_sleep ~ phone_dependence + age + bmi + smoking + drinking + phone_before_bed (\(n = 409\)), and the three students with the largest Cook’s distances.

sleep_glm <- glm(good_sleep ~ phone_dependence + age + bmi + smoking + drinking + phone_before_bed,
                 data = sleep, family = "binomial")
resid_panel(sleep_glm, plots = c("lev", "cookd"), type = "pearson")
sleep_aug <- augment(sleep_glm, data = sleep)
sleep_aug |>
  arrange(desc(.cooksd)) |>
  select(good_sleep, phone_dependence, age, bmi, .hat, .cooksd) |>
  head(3)
# A tibble: 3 × 6
  good_sleep phone_dependence   age   bmi  .hat .cooksd
       <int>            <int> <int> <dbl> <dbl>   <dbl>
1          1               15    76  1.04 0.834  4.13  
2          1               16    16 19.4  0.102  0.0841
3          1               16    18 20.2  0.101  0.0839

  1. Which student has high leverage? Use the table to explain why.

  2. Is this student influential? Explain.

  3. The students in this study were all ages 15 to 21. What would you do with this observation?

  4. Why can’t we use the Pearson \(\chi^2\) goodness-of-fit test to check this model?

TipSolution

  1. The student with age 76 and BMI 1.04 has leverage 0.83, far above the rule of thumb \(2(p+1)/n = 2(7)/409 = 0.034\). Their age and BMI are both far from the rest of the data.

  2. Yes. Their Cook’s distance is 4.13, which is above 1 and about 50 times larger than the next largest value. Including this student changes the fitted model a lot.

  3. An age of 76 (and a BMI of 1.04) isn’t possible in this study, so this is almost certainly a data entry error. We should not use this value as is: we could try to find the correct value, or remove the row, refit the model, and report that we did so.

  4. This is a binary logistic regression (\(n_i = 1\) for every row). The \(\chi^2\) approximation for the goodness-of-fit test needs the counts in each row to be large, so the test is only valid for binomial logistic regression with large enough \(n_i\).

4. Perform hypothesis tests about a single regression coefficient

Example question Use the model below for whether an article is fake, using negative and title_has_excl. Before looking at the data, a researcher hypothesized that articles with more negative sentiment are more likely to be fake, after accounting for whether the title has an exclamation point.

news_add <- glm(type_num ~ negative + title_has_excl, data = news, family = "binomial")
tidy(news_add)
# A tibble: 3 × 5
  term               estimate std.error statistic  p.value
  <chr>                 <dbl>     <dbl>     <dbl>    <dbl>
1 (Intercept)          -1.88      0.502     -3.75 0.000180
2 negative              0.383     0.146      2.61 0.00893 
3 title_has_exclTRUE    2.53      0.786      3.21 0.00131

  1. State the hypotheses for the researcher’s question in words and in notation.

  2. Find the p-value for this test, and give your conclusion in context.

  3. What would the p-value be if the researcher had instead hypothesized that articles with more negative sentiment are less likely to be fake?

TipSolution

  1. \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) (after accounting for exclamation points, negative sentiment is not associated with the odds of an article being fake) vs. \(H_a: \beta_1 > 0\) (more negative sentiment is associated with higher odds of being fake), where \(\beta_1\) is the coefficient for negative.

  2. R gives a two-sided p-value. The test statistic \(z = 2.61\) is in the direction of \(H_a\), so the one-sided p-value is half of R’s: \(0.0089 / 2 = 0.0045\). There is strong evidence that, after accounting for whether the title has an exclamation point, articles with more negative sentiment have higher odds of being fake.

  3. The test statistic is in the opposite direction of this \(H_a\), so the p-value is \(1 - 0.0045 = 0.9955\).

Example question Use the bud_int output from Skill 1. Is there evidence that the association between dose and the chance of dying is different for male and female moths?

2 * (1 - pnorm(1.31))
[1] 0.1901958

  1. State the hypotheses in words and in notation.

  2. Show where the test statistic in the output comes from, and explain how the p-value is calculated.

  3. Give your conclusion in context.

TipSolution

  1. \(H_0: \beta_3 = 0\) (the slope for \(\log_2(\text{dose})\) is the same for males and females) vs. \(H_a: \beta_3 \ne 0\) (the slopes are different), where \(\beta_3\) is the coefficient of the interaction sexM:log2(dose).

  2. \(z = \dfrac{\widehat\beta_3 - 0}{SE(\widehat\beta_3)} = \dfrac{0.353}{0.270} = 1.31\). The p-value is the probability of a \(z\) statistic at least this far from 0 (in either direction) using the \(N(0,1)\) distribution: \(2 P(Z > 1.31) = 0.19\).

  3. The p-value is large, so there is not evidence that the association between dose and the odds of dying is different for male and female moths.

5. Construct and interpret confidence intervals for regression coefficients and linear combinations of regression coefficients

Example question Use the med_glm output from Skill 2, and the qnorm() output below.

qnorm(0.95)
[1] 1.644854

  1. Calculate a 90% confidence interval for the coefficient of GPA.

  2. Interpret your interval on the odds scale, for a 0.1-point increase in GPA.

TipSolution

  1. \(z^* = 1.645\), so the interval is \(4.676 \pm 1.645(1.642) = (1.976, 7.377)\).

  2. For a 0.1-point increase, multiply the endpoints by 0.1 and exponentiate: \((e^{0.1976}, e^{0.7377}) = (1.22, 2.09)\). We are 90% confident that, holding MCAT score constant, a 0.1-point increase in GPA is associated with the odds of acceptance being multiplied by between 1.22 and 2.09.

Example question Use the bud_int model from Skill 1. Below is the covariance matrix of the estimated coefficients.

round(vcov(bud_int), 4)
                (Intercept)    sexM log2(dose) sexM:log2(dose)
(Intercept)          0.3055 -0.3055    -0.0839          0.0839
sexM                -0.3055  0.6058     0.0839         -0.1866
log2(dose)          -0.0839  0.0839     0.0279         -0.0279
sexM:log2(dose)      0.0839 -0.1866    -0.0279          0.0729

  1. Write the slope of \(\log_2(\text{dose})\) for male moths as a linear combination of the \(\beta\)’s, and calculate its estimate.

  2. Calculate the standard error of your estimate in (a).

  3. Calculate a 95% confidence interval for the slope for male moths, and interpret it on the odds scale.

TipSolution

  1. \(\gamma = \beta_2 + \beta_3\), so \(\widehat\gamma = 0.906 + 0.353 = 1.259\).

  2. \(SE(\widehat\gamma) = \sqrt{Var(\widehat\beta_2) + Var(\widehat\beta_3) + 2 Cov(\widehat\beta_2, \widehat\beta_3)} = \sqrt{0.0279 + 0.0729 + 2(-0.0279)} = 0.212\)

  3. \(1.259 \pm 1.96(0.212) = (0.843, 1.675)\). Exponentiating, \((e^{0.843}, e^{1.675}) = (2.32, 5.34)\). We are 95% confident that, for male moths, each doubling of the dose is associated with the odds of dying being multiplied by between 2.32 and 5.34.

6. Use a regression model to make appropriate predictions

Example question Below is the model from the homework for whether a fatal accident was tire-related (Cause), using the age of the vehicle (in years), the number of passengers, and the make of the vehicle (Ford or Other).

tire_glm <- glm(Cause ~ VehicleAge + I(VehicleAge^2) + Passengers * Make,
                data = tires, family = "binomial")
tidy(tire_glm)
# A tibble: 6 × 5

  <chr>                   <dbl>     <dbl>     <dbl>        <dbl>
1 (Intercept)           -10.8       2.23      -4.87 0.00000110  
2 VehicleAge              3.42      1.30       2.62 0.00871

5 MakeOther              -1.53      0.717     -2.14 0.0326      
6 Passengers:MakeOther   -0.636     0.330     -1.93 0.0539

  1. Calculate the predicted log-odds, odds, and probability that an accident in a 3-year-old Ford with 2 passengers was tire-related.

  2. Repeat (a) for a 3-year-old vehicle of another make with 2 passengers.

  3. For a Ford with 2 passengers, at what vehicle age are the predicted odds of a tire-related accident the highest?

TipSolution

  1. \(\widehat\eta = -10.847 + 3.421(3) -0.396(3^2) + 0.839(2) = -2.470\). The predicted odds are \(e^{-2.470} = 0.0846\), and the predicted probability is \(0.0846 / (1 + 0.0846) = 0.0780\).

  2. Add the MakeOther and interaction terms: \(\widehat\eta = -2.470 -1.532 + (-0.636)(2) = -5.274\). The predicted odds are \(e^{-5.274} = 0.0051\), and the predicted probability is 0.0051.

  3. The log-odds is a downward-facing parabola in vehicle age, which is highest at \(-\widehat\beta_1 / (2\widehat\beta_2) = 3.421 / (2 \times 0.396) = 4.32\) years. (This is the same for any make and number of passengers, since age doesn’t interact with them.)

7. Conduct inference to compare nested regression models

Example question For the smartphone and sleep data, we want to know whether smoking and drinking help explain sleep quality, after accounting for smartphone dependence, age, BMI, and smartphone use before bed. Below are the residual deviances of the two models and some \(\chi^2\) critical values.

sleep_reduced <- glm(good_sleep ~ phone_dependence + age + bmi + phone_before_bed,
                     data = sleep, family = "binomial")
sleep_full <- glm(good_sleep ~ phone_dependence + age + bmi + phone_before_bed + smoking + drinking,
                  data = sleep, family = "binomial")

deviance(sleep_reduced)
[1] 546.6407
deviance(sleep_full)
[1] 542.1046
qchisq(0.95, df = 1:3)
[1] 3.841459 5.991465 7.814728

  1. Explain why these two models are nested.

  2. State the null and alternative hypotheses.

  3. Calculate the likelihood ratio test statistic and give its degrees of freedom. What do you conclude at \(\alpha = 0.05\)?

TipSolution

  1. The reduced model is the full model with the coefficients for smoking and drinking set to 0.

  2. \(H_0: \beta_{\text{smoking}} = \beta_{\text{drinking}} = 0\) vs. \(H_a\): at least one of these is not 0 (in the full model).

  3. \(\Lambda = 546.64 - 542.10 = 4.54\) on 2 df. This is less than the critical value of 5.99, so the p-value is larger than 0.05 (it is 0.104). There is not enough evidence that smoking and drinking help explain sleep quality, after accounting for the other variables.

  1. State the hypotheses for the likelihood ratio test, and give your conclusion at \(\alpha = 0.05\).

  2. The SexM row of the coefficient table also tests whether Sex is needed. Compare its p-value to the LRT p-value. Why aren’t they exactly the same?

AIC adds a coefficient whenever the deviance drops by more than 2, while the LRT at \(\alpha = 0.05\) requires a drop of more than 3.84. The drop here (3.23) is in between.

8. Identify a binomial logistic regression scenario and whether overdispersion is present
