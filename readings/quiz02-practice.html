1. Express a MLR model using proper notation

TipSolution

Example question In general, higher temperatures means more bike rides. But, on very hot days, people may be less likely to bike. This relationship can be captured with a quadratic model. Below is a quadratic model for daily rides using temperature.

quad_mod <- lm(rides ~ temp_actual + I(temp_actual^2), data = bikes)

  1. Write down the theoretical model, defining all variables and stating the assumption on the error term.

  2. Write down the fitted model.

  3. Is this still a linear regression model, even though the fitted model is a curve? Explain.

TipSolution

  1. \(Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \beta_2 x_i^2 + \varepsilon_i, \quad \varepsilon_i \overset{iid}{\sim} N(0, \sigma^2)\), where \(Y_i\) is the number of rides on day \(i\) and \(x_i\) is the temperature (degrees F) on day \(i\).

  3. Yes. The model is still linear in the \(\beta\)’s: the mean is a sum of coefficients times (functions of) the predictor. Squaring the predictor changes the shape of the fitted curve, but we still estimate the \(\beta\)’s with least squares, check the same conditions, and do inference the same way. One way to think of it is we are creating a new predictor \(x_2\), where \(x_2 = x_1^2\). Polynomial regression is then still multiple linear regression.

3. Check necessary conditions for a multiple regression model and discuss the implications of a model deficiency

Example question Here are the residual plots for a model of used car Price using Mileage and engine size (Liter). In this data set, the cars are listed in order of make and model (e.g., all of the Buick Centurys are listed together).

liter_mod <- lm(Price ~ Mileage + Liter, data = cars)
resid_panel(liter_mod, type = "standardized")

  1. Which condition(s) look violated? Say which plot(s) you used to tell.

  2. Look at the index plot. What do you think is causing the “runs” of residuals that are all above (or all below) zero? What does this suggest about the model?

  3. Given these violations, can you trust the p-values in the summary() output for this model? Can you trust the estimated coefficients? Explain.

TipSolution

  1. Independence: the index plot shows long runs of residuals on the same side of zero, so residuals for cars that are next to each other in the data are related. Normality: the Q-Q plot curves well above the line in the upper tail, and the histogram is right-skewed (and looks like it has two groups). Equal variance is also questionable: the residual plot has a few groups of points that fan out above the line.

  2. Since the cars are listed by make and model, the runs are groups of cars of the same make/model. Cars of the same model have similar prices, so if the model over- or under-predicts one of them, it does the same for the rest. This suggests the model is missing an important predictor (like Make, or the model of the car), and that the observations aren’t independent given only Mileage and Liter.

  3. No, the p-values aren’t trustworthy. The independence violation means the standard errors are too small (we have less independent information than \(n\) suggests), so the p-values and confidence intervals are too optimistic. The estimated coefficients are also suspect, but for a different reason: if an important predictor is missing, the coefficients for Mileage and Liter may be picking up its effect.

4. Perform hypothesis tests about a single regression coefficient

Example question Use the year_mod output from Skill 1. Is there evidence that the relationship between temperature and ridership changed from 2011 to 2012?

  1. State the null and alternative hypotheses in words and using proper notation.

  2. Show how the test statistic was calculated, and give its degrees of freedom.

  3. Report the p-value and state your conclusion in context.

TipSolution
  1. Using the model from Skill 1, the difference in slopes between 2012 and 2011 is \(\beta_3\) (the interaction coefficient).

\(H_0: \beta_3 = 0\) (the slope for temperature is the same in both years) vs. \(H_A: \beta_3 \ne 0\) (the slope for temperature is different in 2011 and 2012).

  1. \(t = \dfrac{\widehat\beta_3 - 0}{SE(\widehat\beta_3)} = \dfrac{24.174}{8.406} \approx 2.88\), with \(df = n - (p + 1) = 500 - 4 = 496\).

  2. \(p =\) 0.0042 (two-sided, from the output). There is strong evidence that the relationship between temperature and ridership was different in 2012: each additional degree was associated with about 24.2 more additional rides in 2012 than in 2011.

Example question Use the humid_mod output from Skill 2. Before looking at the data, an analyst hypothesized that more humid days have fewer rides, after accounting for temperature and whether it’s a weekend.

  1. State \(H_0\) and \(H_A\) in symbols, matching the analyst’s hypothesis.

  2. Calculate the test statistic.

  3. Is the p-value in the summary() output the right one to use? If not, how would you find the correct p-value? State your conclusion.

TipSolution

  1. Let \(\beta_2\) be the coefficient for humidity. \(H_0: \beta_2 = 0\) vs. \(H_A: \beta_2 < 0\).

  2. \(t = \dfrac{-25.366 - 0}{3.628} \approx -6.99\), with \(df = 500 - 4 = 496\).

  3. No: R reports a two-sided p-value, but the analyst’s alternative is one-sided. Since the test statistic is negative (in the direction of \(H_A\)), the correct p-value is half of R’s. R reports \(p =\) 8.77e-12, so the one-sided p-value is about 4.4e-12. There is very strong evidence that, holding temperature and weekend/weekday constant, more humid days have fewer rides on average.

5. Construct and interpret confidence intervals for regression coefficients and linear combinations of regression coefficients

Example question Use the humid_mod output from Skill 2, and the qt() output below.

qt(.01, df = 496, lower.tail = FALSE)
[1] 2.333889
qt(.025, df = 496, lower.tail = FALSE)
[1] 1.964758
qt(.05, df = 496, lower.tail = FALSE)
[1] 1.647932
qt(.1, df = 496, lower.tail = FALSE)
[1] 1.283261

  1. Construct a 90% confidence interval for the coefficient of humidity.

  2. Interpret your interval in context.

TipSolution
  1. For a 90% interval, there is 5% in each tail, so \(t^* =\) 1.648 (from qt(.05, df = 496, lower.tail = FALSE)).

\(-25.366 \pm 1.648 \times 3.628 \;\Longrightarrow\; (-31.3, \; -19.4)\)

  1. We are 90% confident that, holding temperature and weekend/weekday constant, each additional percentage point of humidity is associated with between 19.4 and 31.3 fewer rides per day, on average.

Example question Use the year_mod model from Skill 1. Below is the covariance matrix of the estimated coefficients.

vcov(year_mod)
                     (Intercept) temp_actual    year2012 temp_actual:year2012
(Intercept)           137019.556 -2129.66456 -137019.556           2129.66456
temp_actual            -2129.665    34.02686    2129.665            -34.02686
year2012             -137019.556  2129.66456  290737.973          -4475.41705
temp_actual:year2012    2129.665   -34.02686   -4475.417             70.66346
qt(.025, df = 496, lower.tail = FALSE)
[1] 1.964758

  1. Write the slope for temperature in 2012 as a linear combination of the model coefficients, and calculate its estimate.

  2. Calculate the standard error of the 2012 slope. Why can’t you just add the two standard errors from the summary() output?

  3. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the 2012 slope, and interpret it in context.

  4. A city planner claims that, in 2012, each additional degree F was associated with 100 more rides per day, on average. Conduct a hypothesis test of this claim.

TipSolution
  1. The 2012 slope is \(\gamma = \beta_1 + \beta_3\) (so \(c_1 = c_2 = 1\)).

\(\widehat\gamma = 71.587 + 24.174 = 95.760\)

  1. \(Var(\widehat\gamma) = Var(\widehat\beta_1) + Var(\widehat\beta_3) + 2\,Cov(\widehat\beta_1, \widehat\beta_3) = 34.027 + 70.663 + 2(-34.027) = 36.637\)

\(SE(\widehat\gamma) = \sqrt{36.637} \approx 6.053\)

We can’t just add the standard errors because (1) variances add, not standard errors, and (2) the two estimates are correlated, so we need the covariance term. Here the covariance is negative, which makes the standard error smaller.

  1. \(95.76 \pm 1.965 \times 6.053 \;\Longrightarrow\; (83.9, \; 107.7)\)

We are 95% confident that, in 2012, each additional degree F was associated with between 83.9 and 107.7 more rides per day, on average.

  1. \(H_0: \beta_1 + \beta_3 = 100\) vs. \(H_A: \beta_1 + \beta_3 \ne 100\).

\(t = \dfrac{\widehat\gamma - 100}{SE(\widehat\gamma)} = \dfrac{95.76 - 100}{6.053} \approx -0.7\), with \(df = 496\).

The p-value is the area in both tails of a \(t\) distribution with 496 df beyond \(\pm 0.7\), which is about 0.48. There is no evidence against the planner’s claim: a 2012 slope of 100 rides per degree is consistent with the data. (We could also see this from the interval in (c), which contains 100.)

6. Use a regression model to make appropriate predictions and communicate the uncertainty of these predictions through an interval

Example question Use the humid_mod model from Skill 2. For a weekend day with a temperature of 80°F and 50% humidity, the predicted number of rides is 4931. R gives these two 95% intervals: (2563, 7299) and (4628, 5233).

  1. Which interval is the confidence interval and which is the prediction interval? How can you tell?

  2. Capital Bikeshare is scheduling staff for next Saturday, which is forecast to be 80°F with 50% humidity. Which interval should they use?

  3. Interpret the interval you did not choose in (b).

TipSolution

  1. (4628, 5233) is the confidence interval and (2563, 7299) is the prediction interval. The prediction interval is wider because it accounts for both the uncertainty in the estimated mean and the variability of one individual day around that mean.

  2. The prediction interval, since they are predicting the number of rides on one specific day.

  3. We are 95% confident that the average number of rides, across all weekend days with a temperature of 80°F and 50% humidity, is between 4628 and 5233.

Example question The plot below shows lung function (FEV, in L/s) vs. Age for the 345 adolescents in the FEV data, along with the fitted SLR line and two shaded bands.

  1. Which band (wide orange or narrow dark) shows the confidence intervals, and which shows the prediction intervals?

  2. Why does it make sense that one band is so much wider than the other?

  3. Where is the narrow dark band narrowest? Why?

TipSolution

  1. The narrow dark band shows the confidence intervals for the mean FEV; the wide orange band shows the prediction intervals for individual adolescents.

  2. The prediction interval includes the person-to-person variability of individual observations around the mean (\(\widehat\sigma\)), in addition to the uncertainty in the estimated line. That extra variability doesn’t shrink with more data, so most of the observed points fall inside the orange band.

  3. It is narrowest near the average age (about 12.1 years). The standard error of the mean response includes \((x_0 - \bar{x})^2\), so we’re most certain about where the line is near the center of the data, and less certain toward the edges (here, the older ages, where there are also fewer observations).

7. Conduct inference to compare nested regression models

Example question In the first week of class, we used the NHANES data to model a depression risk score (dep_score, the log of the PHQ-9 score + 1) using daily sitting time (sedentary_hours). The data also record each participant’s sex. Researchers want to know whether sex is related to the depression score at all (as either a shift in the line, a change in the slope, or both), after accounting for sitting time. They compare these two models:

  • Model 1: dep_score ~ sedentary_hours
  • Model 2: dep_score ~ sedentary_hours * sex
Model Residual df SSE
Model 1: dep_score ~ sedentary_hours 657 512.88
Model 2: dep_score ~ sedentary_hours * sex 655 503.90

  1. Are these models nested? Which is the full model and which is the reduced model?

  2. Write out the full model, then state the null and alternative hypotheses using proper \(\beta\) notation.

  3. Calculate the F-statistic and give its degrees of freedom.

  4. Describe how the p-value is found (what distribution, and which tail?). The output below may help.

pf(5.83, df1 = 2, df2 = 655, lower.tail = FALSE)
[1] 0.003092683
  1. What do you conclude?
TipSolution

  1. Yes. Model 1 is Model 2 with the two sex terms set to 0, so Model 2 is the full model and Model 1 is the reduced model.

  2. Full model: \(\mu\{Y | X\} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{sedentary} + \beta_2 \text{male} + \beta_3 \text{sedentary} \times \text{male}\), where male is an indicator equal to 1 for male participants and 0 for female participants (the reference level).

\(H_0: \beta_2 = \beta_3 = 0\) (sex is not related to the depression score after accounting for sitting time) vs. \(H_A:\) at least one of \(\beta_2, \beta_3 \ne 0\).

  1. \(d = 657 - 655 = 2\) coefficients are being tested.

\(F = \dfrac{(SSE_{reduced} - SSE_{full})/d}{SSE_{full}/df_{full}} = \dfrac{(512.88 - 503.9)/2}{503.9/655} \approx 5.83\)

with 2 and 655 degrees of freedom.

  1. The p-value is the area in the upper tail of an \(F\) distribution with 2 and 655 degrees of freedom, above 5.83. (Only large values of \(F\) are evidence against \(H_0\).) From the output, \(p \approx 0.0031\).

  2. There is strong evidence that sex is related to the depression risk score after accounting for sitting time (through a shift in the line, a change in the slope, or both).

Example question Consider these four models for the bikes data:

  • Model A: rides ~ temp_actual
  • Model B: rides ~ temp_actual + humidity
  • Model C: rides ~ temp_actual * year + humidity
  • Model D: rides ~ humidity + weekend

For each comparison, say whether the most appropriate test is a t-test, an extra-sums-of-squares F-test, or neither, and briefly explain.

  1. Model A vs. Model B

  2. Model A vs. Model C

  3. Model B vs. Model D

  4. The summary() output for Model C ends with an F-statistic. Which two models does that F-test compare?

TipSolution

  1. A t-test for the humidity coefficient in Model B. The models differ by one coefficient, so the t-test and the F-test are the same test (\(F = t^2\)).

  2. An extra-sums-of-squares F-test. Model A is nested in Model C, and the models differ by three coefficients (year2012, the temp_actual:year2012 interaction, and humidity), which a single t-test can’t test at once.

  3. Neither. The models are not nested (each has a predictor the other doesn’t), so neither test applies.

  4. Model C vs. the intercept-only model (\(\mu\{Y|X\} = \beta_0\)). It tests whether any of the predictors in Model C are useful.

A. A 1-mile increase in mileage is associated with a 0.087 dollar decrease in price, on average. B. A 1% increase in mileage is associated with an estimated 0.087% decrease in price. C. A 1% increase in mileage is associated with an estimated 8.7% decrease in price. D. A 1-mile increase in mileage is associated with an \(e^{-0.087}\) dollar decrease in price, on average.

  1. Suppose we had used \(\sqrt{\text{Price}}\) as the response instead. Could we interpret the slope on the original scale (dollars) the same way? Why or why not?
TipSolution

  1. Doubling the mileage increases \(\log(\text{Mileage})\) by \(\log(2) \approx 0.693\). So doubling the mileage is associated with a change in average price of about \(-2222 \times 0.693 \approx -1540\) dollars, i.e., a decrease of about $1,540.

  2. B. In a log-log model, the slope is (approximately) the percent change in \(y\) for a 1% increase in \(x\). (More precisely, a 1% increase in mileage multiplies the predicted price by \(1.01^{-0.087} \approx 0.99914\).)

  3. No. There’s no simple back-transformation for a square root that turns a constant change in \(\sqrt{\text{Price}}\) into a constant change (or a constant percent change) in price. We could still use the model for predictions (by squaring the predicted values), but the slope doesn’t have a clean interpretation on the original scale. That’s one reason log transformations are so popular.

  5. Not really. Multicollinearity makes the individual coefficients unstable and hard to interpret, but the predictions can still be good, as long as the cars we predict for have the same relationship between Liter and Cyl as the cars in the data.
