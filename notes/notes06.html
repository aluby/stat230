Back-transforming

Converting a transformed variable back to its original scale.

Example: back-transforming a mean

Mean of log(Time) = 2.146.

Back-transformed mean: \(e^{2.146} \approx\) 8.55.

NoteR note

log() is the natural log; exp(x) calculates \(e^x\). Note: back-transforming the mean of the logs does not generally equal the mean on the original scale (98.56) – it estimates the geometric mean/median instead.

Displaying a transformed model

gf_lm(formula = log(y) ~ log(x), backtrans = exp, interval = "confidence") plots the fitted line and CI back-transformed onto the original scale, so you can show results in units your audience understands.

Rules of thumb

  • Transform X: if the mean function is nonlinear, but monotonic, and the residual variance is constant.
  • Transform Y: if the mean function is nonlinear and residual variance increases as the mean increases (log, reciprocal, or square root often work).
  • Log rule: if values range over more than 1 order of magnitude and are strictly positive, the natural log is likely helpful.
  • Range rule: if the range is considerably less than 1 order of magnitude, transformations are unlikely to help.
  • Square roots are useful for count data.

Ladder of transformations & Rule of the Bulge

Tukey and Mosteller’s “Rule of the Bulge” gives a visual guide: match the direction your scatterplot bulges to the ladder position, and move X and/or Y toward that end to straighten the relationship.

Issues with transformations

  • You’re often guessing – statistics is an art and a science.
  • Transformations change the ___________________________ of the parameters – you need to back-transform to give interpretable results.
  • Transformations change the SEs of the parameters.
  • Not always easy to keep track of all your assumptions once you’ve transformed.
